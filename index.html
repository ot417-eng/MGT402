§1Part I, context

The rise of AI virtual influencers

A virtual influencer is a fictional, computer-generated persona run by a company. It posts, endorses products and builds a following the way a human creator does.

India's advertising self-regulator, ASCI, defines virtual influencers as fictional computer-generated people or avatars with the realistic features and personalities of humans, who behave like influencers (ASCI, 2023). The definition matters because it carries a duty: in India, such a persona must tell its audience it is not a real person.

How a persona is built

From 2016

CGI and 3D modelling

Artists model a face and body by hand. Imma, Japan's best-known virtual human, is a computer-generated face composited onto photos of a real human model (Bloomberg, 2020).

From 2022

Generative AI

Image and video models produce posts from prompts and reference images. Barcelona agency The Clueless built Aitana López this way (Entrepreneur, 2023).

From 2024

Synthetic voice and conversation

Designed or cloned voices and chat models let a persona talk to followers in real time. Imma's maker, Aww Inc., has focused on conversational-AI virtual humans since 2024 (MarkeZine, 2025).

Why brands use them

Control, cost and constant content

The Clueless created Aitana because human influencers were costly and unreliable, and says AI models let smaller brands afford influencer marketing (Fortune, 2023).

  • Full creative control and brand safety
  • Content around the clock at low marginal cost
  • Novelty and high engagement with young audiences
  • A persona the brand can hold as a long-term asset
The central problem

Many makers, no default owner

A human influencer owns their identity by default. A virtual influencer is assembled by a designer, an agency, an AI platform and a paying brand, and no single law says who owns the result.

This study asks whether copyright, trademark, personality rights, advertising rules and contracts can answer that question.

What this means for the law

The more realistic the persona, the more it persuades, and the greater the risk of deceiving consumers or copying a real person's likeness.

§3Part I, context

The ownership problem

A virtual influencer is a bundle of separate rights: design, name, content, voice, data and goodwill. Each can belong to a different party.

Three hard questions this creates

Design or output?

Owning the original artwork does not mean owning every image an AI later generates of that character.

Character or identity?

Copyright protects creative work; personality rights protect real people. A fictional persona has none of its own, but the people it resembles do.

Law or contract?

Because the law leaves gaps, contracts and platform terms decide ownership in practice. Whoever drafted the contract usually owns the persona.

§4Part II, the law

Copyright: who owns the character and its content?

Copyright clearly protects a character a human designed. Protection for AI-generated posts depends on the country: India credits the person who “causes” the work, the US requires real human authorship, and China has protected a prompted image.

What copyright can protect in a persona

Artistic worksCharacter sheets, face and body design, outfits, logo
Literary worksBackstory, captions, scripts, lyrics
Films and recordingsVideos, music, voice recordings
Computer programs3D model code, rigs, custom tools

Copyright protects expression, not ideas. “A pink-haired virtual model from Tokyo” is free for anyone to use; a specific design is not.

India's AI-authorship record so far

  1. 2020–21
    RAGHAV and Suryast

    Lawyer Ankit Sahni registered an AI-assisted artwork naming himself and his AI tool RAGHAV as co-authors. The Copyright Office then issued a withdrawal notice questioning RAGHAV's legal status (Managing IP).

  2. 8 Dec 2025
    DPIIT Working Paper, Part 1

    A government committee proposed a mandatory blanket licence for AI training, with royalties paid through a central body (Ikigai Law via Mondaq).

  3. 31 Aug 2026
    The DABUS order

    The Registrar of Copyrights held Stephen Thaler's AI-generated artwork original, but ruled that the AI system DABUS cannot be an author because it is not a natural or juristic person. Thaler, who caused the work, could be (Business Standard; LiveLaw). It is an administrative order, not a court ruling.

  4. Pending
    DPIIT Working Paper, Part 2

    The paper on authorship and ownership of AI outputs was due in early 2026 and remained unpublished as of October 2026 (Digital Watch).

Five jurisdictions compared

The person who causes the work is the author

s.2(d)(vi) gives computer-generated works a human author. The 2026 DABUS order confirms an AI cannot be that author, but leaves open which human “causes” a work made by several hands.

Key authorityCopyright Act 1957, s.2(d)(vi)Registrar of Copyrights, DABUS order, 31 Aug 2026

Only human authorship counts

Prompts alone, even detailed or repeated, are not enough. Human selection, arrangement and modification of AI output can be protected.

Key authorityUS Copyright Office, AI Report Part 2, 29 Jan 2025Thaler v. Perlmutter, D.C. Cir., 18 Mar 2025Supreme Court denied review, 2 Mar 2026

The person who made the arrangements, for now

s.9(3) CDPA 1988 gives computer-generated works an author: the person who made the necessary arrangements. The government's March 2026 report proposes repealing it while keeping protection for AI-assisted human work.

Key authorityCopyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988, s.9(3)HSF Kramer on the March 2026 report

A prompted image can be protected

A Stable Diffusion image was protected because the user chose over 150 prompts, ordered them and set parameters. The user is the author; the model's designers are not.

Key authorityLi v. Liu, Beijing Internet Court, 27 Nov 2023

Originality assumes a human mind

Originality means the author's own intellectual creation, which presupposes a human. There is no AI-specific authorship rule.

Key authorityCJEU case law, e.g. Infopaq (2009)

Applied to AVA

  • The original design belongs to Riya, the designer, unless she is an employee or signed a written assignment.
  • Each AI-generated post: in India, whoever “caused” it is the author, but the law does not say whether that is the prompt-writer, the agency or the platform. In the US, a purely prompted post may have no copyright, so anyone could copy it.
  • Design copyright does not cover every output. PixelMint can sue over posts that copy AVA's protected design, but cannot claim copyright in an AI image that only resembles her style.
  • Infringement runs both ways. If the AI tool reproduces someone else's protected work in AVA's content, the agency and the brand may both be liable.

Our assessment

s.2(d)(vi) looks forward-thinking, but it was added in 1994 for simple computer programs. With several contributors, “the person who causes” is unclear, which is why contracts, not the statute, decide ownership in practice.

§5Part II, the law

Trademark and passing off: the persona as a brand

Trademark is the most reliable way to own a virtual influencer's commercial identity. It protects the name and look as a source of goods and services, and it lasts as long as it is used and renewed.

Trade Marks Act 1999, s.2(1)(m) and s.2(1)(zb)

What a persona can register

  • Word mark: the persona's name, such as “AVA”
  • Device mark: a stylised face, silhouette or logo
  • A slogan or catchphrase used in campaigns
  • Classes 9 (downloadable images, virtual goods), 25 (clothing), 35 (advertising), 41 (entertainment and online content)
  • Registration lasts 10 years and renews indefinitely (s.25)

Character merchandising in India

No dedicated statute

Protection comes from copyright in the artwork, trademark protection for the name and distinctive elements, and judge-made passing-off and personality doctrines (King Stubb & Kasiva, 2026). Toys and figurines can also be protected under the Designs Act, 2000 (Managing IP).

Risk in the other direction

Infringing someone else's mark

  • A persona that resembles a mascot or well-known mark can be sued; well-known marks are protected even across unrelated goods (s.11(2)).
  • Generative AI can reproduce logos or branded products in posts without anyone intending it.
  • A clearance search before naming and launching is essential.

Why trademark matters most

Copyright in AI-generated posts is uncertain; a registered name is not. Whoever registers the mark controls the persona commercially. If a designer files first, the agency may have to buy back its own influencer's name. Imma shows the upside: she launched her own label, Astral Body, moving from face of other brands to brand owner.

§6Part II, the law

Personality rights: can a digital identity be owned or imitated?

Personality rights protect real people, not fictional ones. A virtual influencer has none of its own, but it can infringe the rights of any real person whose face, voice, name or mannerisms it copies.

These rights let a person control commercial use of their name, image, likeness, voice, signature, catchphrases and mannerisms. India has no statute: courts derive them from the right to privacy and dignity under Article 21 (K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India, SC 2017; R. Rajagopal v. State of Tamil Nadu, SC 1994), combined with passing off, trademark and copyright (Mondaq). The claimant must be identifiable from the infringing use, so protection mainly helps people whose identity has commercial value.

Implication 1

A fictional persona has no personality rights

AVA cannot sue over “her” identity. PixelMint must rely on copyright, trademark and contract.

Implication 2

Resembling a real person is the largest risk

If AVA's face, voice or catchphrases evoke a celebrity, that person can obtain an injunction quickly, as Anil Kapoor, Jackie Shroff and Arijit Singh did.

Implication 3

Hidden humans have rights too

Body models (as with Imma), voice actors and people whose photos trained a model may have claims. After AI rapper FN Meka was dropped, rapper Kyle the Hooligan publicly claimed he was its original voice.

Implication 4

Platforms are now in the frame

Courts direct Google, YouTube and chatbot hosts to remove content, so “the AI made it” is no defence for an agency.

Our assessment

Indian protection is court-made, celebrity-focused and mostly interim. Ordinary people whose likeness is used to build a persona have far weaker practical protection, and no court has yet ruled finally on a virtual influencer that resembles, rather than copies, a real person.

§7Part II, the law

Advertising rules and AI labels

Without a human influencer, liability does not disappear. India, the US and the EU all treat a virtual influencer's post as an endorsement and place responsibility on the brand and the people who control the persona.

India moved first

ASCI's 2021 guidelines made India the first national regulator to address virtual influencers expressly, two years before the US FTC (InfoLawGroup). As a persona is not a legal person, the duties fall on the entity that controls its posts (Nishith Desai Associates).

United States

FTC Endorsement Guides, 16 CFR Part 255 (2023)

  • An endorser is anyone who is or appears to be an individual, group or institution, which covers virtual influencers (Kirkland & Ellis).
  • Advertisers, endorsers and intermediaries such as ad agencies can all be liable (Osborne Clarke).
  • A persona cannot use a product, so it should not claim personal experience of one.
European Union

AI Act, Article 50, in force from 2 Aug 2026

  • Deployers must disclose deepfakes, and people must be told when they interact with an AI system.
  • The 2026 Digital Omnibus did not delay this; it only gave generative systems already on the market until 2 Dec 2026 for machine-readable marking (Ropes & Gray).
  • A persona that chats with EU followers needs an AI-interaction notice.

Our assessment

Disclosure rules solve deception, answering “is this real?”, but not ownership. They also add a compliance cost human influencers do not face: a double label, paid partnership plus not a real human.

§8Part II, the law

Contracts and licensing: how ownership is actually decided

Where the statute is silent, the real owner of a virtual influencer is whoever the contracts say it is. A well-drafted chain of agreements allocates control, but it cannot create rights the law does not recognise.

The contract chain around AVA

With the designer

Design agreement

Written assignment of copyright in designs, models and future variations; moral-rights undertaking; credit; fee.

With in-house staff

Employment contract

Confirms work is made in the course of employment, so the employer owns it (s.17).

With the AI provider

Platform terms

Who owns outputs; commercial use; whether inputs train the model; indemnities.

PixelMint agency holds AVA
With models and voice actors

Likeness release

Consent to use face, body or voice; scope; duration; payment; warranty of no imitation.

With Threadline

Brand licence

Licensed use of persona and content; term, territory, media, exclusivity, approvals, ASCI and CCPA compliance.

With Instagram, YouTube

Account terms

Account ownership and access, follower data, and the platform's own licence over posts.

Twelve clauses every persona contract needs

  • Ownership and assignmentAssign all copyright in writing and signed (s.19); state term and territory, or the defaults of 5 years and India apply.
  • Future worksCover later AI-generated variations; an assignment of a future work takes effect when it exists (s.18).
  • Moral rightsDesigners keep attribution and integrity rights (s.57); get an undertaking not to object to changes.
  • TrademarkThe agency files and owns the name and logo; the designer agrees not to register similar marks.
  • AI use and provenanceList approved tools; log prompts, edits and human input to support authorship claims.
  • Real-likeness warrantyCreators warrant the persona copies no real face, voice or mark, and indemnify if it does.
  • Licence scope for brandsCampaign-specific, time-limited and territorial; no right to modify or spin off characters.
  • Morality and reputationBoth ways: the brand can exit after a scandal; the agency can refuse content that harms the persona.
  • ComplianceASCI clause 1.4, CCPA guidelines and IT Rules labels, and who bears the penalties.
  • Termination and exitWho keeps the character, account, followers and model files when a relationship ends.
  • Liability and indemnityFor IP infringement, misleading claims and data breaches, including under the DPDP Act 2023 if real people's photos or voices are used.
  • DisputesGoverning law, jurisdiction and arbitration, with a carve-out for urgent injunctions.

What a contract cannot do

It binds only its parties, so it cannot stop a stranger copying an AI post that has no copyright. A post-termination non-compete on a designer is likely void in India under s.27 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, so restrict use of the specific persona instead. And platform terms are non-negotiable for most agencies.

Own the persona or license it?

Brand owns the persona outrightBrand licenses from an agency
ControlFull; the persona becomes a brand asset, like a mascotLimited to the licence

Our answer: licensing suits most brands. Outright ownership makes sense only when the persona is central to the brand's identity and the brand can manage IP in-house.

§9Part III, evidence

Critical analysis: controversies and gaps

Existing law answers the easy cases: copying a celebrity, copying a registered name, undisclosed adverts. It leaves the hard ones open: who owns AI-generated content, who owns a persona built by many hands, and who answers for the harm a persona causes.

Our five arguments

  1. Owning creative work and owning an identity are different things.Copyright and trademark tell us who owns AVA; personality rights tell us whose identity AVA must not take.
  2. Many contributors create overlapping claims.Designer, prompt team, agency, platform and real-life reference people can each claim part of one persona.
  3. Existing law is adequate for some disputes, not all.Courts handle celebrity imitation well; the law is unsettled on AI-output authorship and multi-party creation.
  4. Control comes with risk.Brands choose personas to avoid human unpredictability, yet every incident above shows they inherit legal, reputational and ethical risk instead.
  5. No human influencer does not mean no responsibility.ASCI, CCPA, the FTC and the EU AI Act all place liability on the people behind the persona.

The counter-view

  • The US Copyright Office concluded existing law can resolve AI copyrightability without new legislation (Akin Gump).
  • India's government told the Rajya Sabha in February 2024 that the existing framework is adequate, and the 2026 DABUS order read s.2(d)(vi) as sufficient (Intepat).
  • On this view, contracts plus existing law are enough, and new laws risk chilling innovation.

Our assessment

The law is adequate for disputes between parties who signed contracts. It is inadequate for third parties, meaning copiers, imitated individuals and consumers, and for content with no clear human author.

A persona can hold brand value on its own, but its rights can sit in different hands; here one company held them all, which is why the sale worked. Brud's internal contracts are not public, so we do not claim to know who owns what inside the company.

Anil Kapoor v. Simply Life India, Delhi High Court, 2023

This order marks the boundary for virtual influencers in India. Building an original persona is lawful; building one from a real person's face, voice or catchphrase without consent can be stopped at once.

CS(COMM) 652/2023Interim order, 20 Sept 2023Permanent decree, 30 Apr 2024
Jhakaas

Kapoor first used the catchphrase in the 1985 film Yudh. The court treated it as exclusively associated with him and protected it alongside his name, voice and image (Telangana Today).

The court's reasoning

  • Free speech is protected, but using it becomes illegal when it crosses the line and harms personality rights (Storyboard18).
  • The court said it could not turn a blind eye to commercial misuse of a personality.
  • Endorsements are a celebrity's livelihood, and unlawful use destroys that right; commentators read the order through the right to livelihood (SpicyIP).
  • The court recognised that generative AI and machine learning enable misuse of a persona online.

What it means for virtual influencers

A persona must not be built from a real person's identity without a licence, including voice, catchphrases and mannerisms. Courts act fast and ex parte, and platforms can be ordered to take content down overnight. The case concerns a real person's rights, not ownership of a fictional character: it defines what a virtual influencer must avoid, not who owns one.

§12Part IV, implications

What this means for business

A virtual influencer is an IP portfolio to be managed, not a free substitute for human talent. Its value depends on clean ownership, and the risk sits with whoever pays for and controls the persona.

India

₹1,275 crore to ₹2,800 crore

India's influencer market in 2022, and its projected size by 2027, according to the Department of Consumer Affairs (Inc42).

Worldwide

Nearly 30% less brand interest

Brand interest in virtual influencers fell from 2024 to 2025 even as forecasts stayed bullish (Digiday). Treat growth projections with caution.

What each stakeholder should do

Clear the rights before the campaign

  • Verify who owns the persona's design, name, content and accounts.
  • Get written rights for each campaign, medium, territory and term.
  • Use the double disclosure required by ASCI, CCPA and the IT Rules.
  • Avoid personal-experience claims a persona cannot truthfully make.
  • Prepare exit, morality and crisis clauses.
WhyBrands carry primary consumer-law liability, and the backlash in every incident in §9 landed on the brand.

Hold every right in the company's name

  • Get signed assignments from every freelancer, and file trademarks early.
  • Keep provenance records of human input and the AI tools used.
  • Screen designs and voices against real people and existing marks.
  • Hold the account, follower data and model files in the company's name.
  • Build labels, metadata and approvals into the content workflow.
WhyLil Miquela could be sold with Brud because one company held all the rights.

Negotiate before handing over the files

  • Choose assignment or licence, and price each accordingly.
  • Agree credit, and royalties on future variations.
  • Keep the right to show the work in a portfolio.
  • Know that moral rights survive assignment but are hard to enforce.
WhyWithout a signed assignment a freelance designer keeps copyright (s.17, s.19), which is a strong bargaining position.

Make terms and takedowns clear

  • State plainly who owns outputs and whether commercial use is allowed.
  • Run complaint and takedown processes for likeness and IP claims.
  • Build safeguards against cloning real voices and faces.
WhyThe IT Rules 2026 and court orders since 2023 put platforms in the chain of responsibility.

Recommendations

India needs targeted clarification, not a new IP right for personas: settle who “causes” an AI output, codify personality rights for everyone, and make disclosure and provenance standard practice.

§15Part IV, implications

Conclusion: who owns a digital persona?

No one, by default. Its design, name, content and commercial identity are separate rights spread across several parties, and in practice whoever holds the contracts and the trademark controls it.

Finding 1

A bundle, not an image

Copyright in design and content, trademark in name and look, and contractual control of accounts and deals.

Finding 2

Ownership is distributed

Designers, agencies, platforms, brands and real-life reference people can each hold a part. Lil Miquela shows the bundle can be consolidated and sold.

Finding 3

The law is half ready

Indian courts protect real identities quickly, and the Copyright Office confirmed in 2026 that an AI cannot be an author. Multi-party ownership of AI content remains unresolved.

Finding 4

Responsibility stays with people

ASCI, CCPA, the IT Rules 2026, the FTC and the EU AI Act all hold the humans behind the persona liable.

Final argument

The future of virtual-influencer marketing depends on clear ownership, licensing and accountability as much as on better technology. Until the law catches up, contracts and transparency are what turn an influencer who doesn't exist into an asset someone can legitimately own.

Who owns AVA?

Activity 1, ownership dispute

The facts

  1. PixelMint, a Mumbai agency, pays freelance designer Riya to create AVA. Riya is paid by invoice; nothing is signed.
  2. PixelMint makes AVA's posts with a commercial AI image tool.
  3. PixelMint registers the trademark “AVA” in Classes 25, 35 and 41.
  4. Fashion brand Threadline licenses AVA for a six-month campaign.
  5. Six months later, Riya leaves and says she owns AVA.

1. Who owns AVA's original design?

Riya. A freelancer keeps copyright unless she assigns it in writing and signs (s.17, s.19). Payment alone is not enough.

2. Who owns AVA's AI-generated posts?

Arguably PixelMint. In India the person who causes a computer-generated work is its author (s.2(d)(vi)), subject to the AI tool's terms. In the US only human-authored elements would be protected.

3. Can Riya keep using the AVA character?

Her design, not the name. PixelMint owns the registered mark for similar goods and services. The likely outcome is a negotiated buy-out of Riya's copyright.

4. Does Threadline keep rights after the campaign?

Only the licence. Threadline's rights end with the six-month term unless the contract says otherwise.

5. What should PixelMint have done?

A signed assignment. It should also have added an AI-output clause and an exit clause. Filing the trademark early was the one thing it got right.
